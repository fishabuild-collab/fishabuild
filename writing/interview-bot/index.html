I built a bot that interviews me, so my work stops dying in my notes

Writing · 2026

The problem: I do the work, learn a lot, and then the story disappears into a one-line note. What I did: I built an AI interviewer that pulls the story out of me in small bites and turns it into a case study draft. Result: three projects have gone through it, all ready to publish, and the first one is live on my site. This is a case study about the thing that wrote it.

Why I built it

I always wanted a record of what I learn and what I build. The blocker was simple: sitting down to write a full case study is hard, especially in a very busy job.

I got the idea while working on an international campaign award submission. Seeing how agencies structure these stories made me wonder if I could scale the idea down to bite size for personal use. If I could answer questions on the train, over coffee, or yes, in the restroom, I might actually get the stories out.

What I tried first

Two things, and both failed.

Notes and messages to myself. They got lost, scattered, and never had any structure. When I needed to pull a portfolio together, I was piecing it from random files. The result was about 10 pages, visual-heavy, with very little of the thinking behind the work.

Letting AI draft the whole thing alone. It came out too AI to read, and it had none of my perspective. Nobody wants to read that, including me.

So the AI couldn't write it for me. It had to ask me, and the writing had to come from my answers.

How it works

The pipeline is simple:

  1. Do the real work.
  2. Capture it in my own notes system.
  3. The bot interviews me.
  4. I get a draft.
  5. I review, strip, and confirm.
  6. I publish to my site.

The bot is a prompt in a Claude project, so it's one tap away. In the interview it asks one question at a time and reflects back what it heard. It pushes for evidence and for what I personally owned, and it has a few commands: recap, skip, and draft. What comes back is a solid markdown case study with my own quotes in it, ready for my site.

The part I gated on purpose

When you talk freely about a project, venting comes out. So does the messy side, like tough conversations and the internal context that isn't mine to share. I want the raw version to be honest, because that's where the real thinking is.

So I built a publishing-safety gate. The interview can be messy, but the output is clean. It strips names, internal friction, and confidential details, and it flags anything it isn't sure about instead of guessing. The value is in the build story, the problem-solving, and the results, so those are what stay.

The questions I'd never ask myself

This is what makes it better than a generic "describe your project" prompt. Two examples:

"Who would you sell this to?" I wouldn't think to ask that about my own builds. It makes me see whether a one-off thing could become something with a future.

"How bad is it if this goes wrong, or if you don't make the decision?" I never visualize the impact of a mistake. When I'm forced to, I also see how much my work was actually holding up. That's the opposite of how I usually talk about it, which is "it was nothing."

A worked example: the note vs. the story

The first project I ran through it was a 48-hour floor activation for an event. Left alone, my write-up would have been one line: "Hard timeline, we made it through, learning: don't measure things alone." That's true, and it's useless.

Here's what the interview drilled out of it.

The risk. If I had waited for final fixes on the artwork, the stickers wouldn't have been printed in time, and the event would have had no visual presence on the floor.

The decision. I picked up the final fixes myself and pushed through some of the calls. For certain files, I decided there was no time for another round of review. I triaged by risk. Typos, alignment, QR codes, and sizes had to be right. Everything else was minor at that point.

The cost. Some sizing was off, and I had to fix sizing on almost every file at the end. That came from the timeline, not from the number of review rounds. It's still a real lesson.

The lesson. Next time I'd find a partner for accurate measurement instead of measuring alone for a quick quote.

That one-liner and this version describe the same job. The difference is that the bot made me go through the in-between steps I usually skip. I jump to "result and learning," which makes the learning hollow and easy to forget. The interview makes me earn the lesson first.

Keeping it a tool, not a hobby

Building and polishing a system like this can easily become procrastination, and I know it. Two things keep me honest.

First, I gave myself a real deadline to get my site up, and that kept me from over-building the bot.

Second, I set a hard rule: one system improvement per case study, at most. If I want to change the bot more than that, I'm procrastinating, and I should go ship something instead.

Results so far

  • Three projects run through the bot, all ready to publish. The 48-hour floor activation is live.
  • About 2 days from first run to a live site, not counting busy work days (estimate).
  • Up to 20 minutes of actual interviewing per case study (estimate). Because I do it in fragments between a demanding job, the elapsed time is closer to 2 days.

The results I can't put a number on matter more to me. I now have something to look back on, with the thinking in it. And when the voice in my head says my work isn't that good, I can point to a published site. The evidence is public, and it's hard to argue with.

What it doesn't do (yet)

I'm still exploring where it falls short. Three runs isn't enough for me to claim I know its weaknesses, and I'd rather say that than invent some. Keep growing is the point.

Where it goes next

My one planned improvement is an emotional log. I want it to track what excites me, what drains me, and how my feelings about work move over time, so it can help point me toward a happier career.

As for a product, I'm not sure. I don't think it's a big enough idea on its own, unless I turn it into an end-to-end portfolio system. What I do want is to build a community, and this is a good giveaway for it: quick prompts people can use right away.

Reflection and one takeaway

The biggest thing I got from this is perspective. I'm learning more because I'm being asked to slow down on the parts I normally skip, and I get a clearer view of my own values from it.

Some use cases are easy to set up but life changing. This is one of them.

If you want to steal this: copy the idea, not my tool. Write a prompt that makes your AI interview you, one question at a time, and set it up as a custom bot in whatever AI you use. Don't let it write for you. Make it ask you, then write from your answers.
